## Per-speaker breakdown

Quotes are verbatim from the **automated** transcript (fillers dropped, `…`

### Ben Ballingall

**What Flux is and where it came from**

- "A political party that believes in or proposes issue based direct democracy.
  So that's a way for all the citizens… to vote on the issues that come up in
  parliament and have those results aggregated and shown to a representative.
  And our representatives will do what their community votes" (bonus 00:00:53).
  "A very straight up and down democracy, the people voting on every single
  issue."
- Founders "Max Kane" (Max Kaye) and Nathan Spataro, "middle of 2015": they
  "had the ability and the code behind being able to develop the app… used…
  that to generate enough buzz to get the party registered before the 2016
  federal election" (bonus 00:01:45).
- Why: "A lot of the flaws that we face politically… come from a lack of
  representation, a lack of transparency, and a lack of accountability" (bonus
  00:02:36). "Technological innovation has sort of always progressed some sort
  of political change… ours just happens to be based on blockchain tech and the
  internet."

**What Flux doesn't solve**

- "Flux is one element… of designing an open democracy… What it doesn't sort
  of, or what will grow, hopefully, symbiotically is how are people going to be
  informed about these decisions… once we've released all these old political
  parties of leading us, how do we decide what facets of their ideology were
  good and what were bad?" (bonus 00:03:28–00:04:20).
- "That's the million, billion, trillion dollar question. And you know, we don't
  know… we think of this voting for a team… this tribal team… with these 50
  points" (bonus 00:05:11).
- "It's totally acceptable to only like one initiative that the Greens put
  forward. And then the next week to like a One Nation initiative… they can both
  be right. And in today's politics… that's completely… absurd. So that's a huge
  change that isn't solved by people being able to vote" (bonus 00:06:07).

**The app** (bonus 00:07:04–00:08:51)

- Precondition: "a Flux MP has been voted in in your electorate". Log in with
  "your federal electoral [roll] details… verified by the government that you
  are one person"; see upcoming bills with countdowns; read a synopsis and the
  parties' material; vote yes, no, or not at all; see your electorate's result;
  "post to Twitter, post to Facebook, email my representative".
- "This is definitely a level where we don't want to be proposing any
  information, so you won't find information from Flux."

**Security** (bonus 00:08:51–00:11:27)

- On the February 2020 Iowa caucus app: "that exact fault was an actual fault in
  the code… But it'll still, in people's psyche, just be an app broke and it
  screwed up the vote."
- "It is blockchain based voting. So you know the ledger is immutable. We do have
  the strongest security and anonymization in industry… It's the same stuff that
  protects your bank… it's open source. You can read it. If it's got a flaw,
  people will find it. And right now, we are telling you, there are no flaws."
- "It's one of those ones you really just have to put up and be like, it works,
  and you can't prove us wrong. And the people that can prove us wrong agree with
  us." **A strong claim; attribute it, and don't let a post present it as
  settled.** Contrast Andrew Kay in the [Trust episode](2019-12-11_trust-concept-analysis.md):
  internet voting is "the worst thing I can think of".

**Brexit** (main 00:00:52–00:03:37)

- "He's seen Brexit as this terrible example of democracy that went wrong. But…
  even just bringing up that, you know, who made up the question? What was the
  wording of the question? Like, why… did it take two years to implement?"
- "It's the first time people have had a referendum in decades. How many people
  actually really, truly thought about the reaction and the result?"

**IBDD** (main 00:04:29–00:09:51)

- Three parts: direct democracy ("being able to vote yes or no"), liquid
  democracy ("you can delegate on that one issue or in that one field"), and
  vote-saving ("the ability to not vote at all… store up a little bit of that
  vote's energy… put it towards your own novel idea").
- The delegation trap: "People are just going to keep going to the same leader…
  Dr. Karl is my science and tech guy, and he always will be for the next 20
  years… but he might not still have the best ideas. So what you actually want
  is a way for people to specialise and hyper specialise."
- "We don't want to incentivise people just giving away their vote."
- On bundled ideologies: "If you wanted to have support for all of your other 49
  values, you have to, by default, support that other one." Brian's cable-TV
  analogy ("Oh, thank you for giving me that analogy rather than me tiptoeing
  through Christian values"): specialise in the sports package and be "rewarded
  by getting more political energy that you can spend on sports related things".
- "Liquid democracy is just one facet within IBDD… the three keys, direct
  democracy, liquid democracy, and the issue based vote sharing, they all serve
  different purposes. They're not necessarily like a linear thing" (00:13:41).

**Citizens' juries** (main 00:09:51–00:12:49)

- "I was jokingly being a little rough on Citizen Juries." Both "are trying to fix
  bad representation… They empower ordinary people… But what they don't do is
  they don't reward specialization, and they don't reward someone getting the
  correct answer. Someone doing really well in a Citizen Jury may never be called
  up again."
- "We don't want to try and create these representatives that are really great at
  most things, or jack of all trades… when we could have masters at everything,
  delivering the results that are… confirmed by the people."
- Brian's patent-jury example: jurors "can be bamboozled or just jargoned… You
  couldn't do [that] to a specialist."
- Brian presses: what stops a delegate being corrupted, as politicians are? Ben:
  "they can either get lazy or they can be corrupted. The best thing about… a
  digital liquid democracy is the second they do that, you pull that power back."

**Adoption and agenda** (main 00:15:35–00:17:18)

- "Get a member… elected… into parliament… Secondary to that… an app that allows
  people to mirror parliament and have their vote and then have it displayed
  according to their community… as a method of people lobbying their
  representatives."
- With one MP, "a lot of the time we're just straight reacting to other parties".
  Banked votes: "50 votes and that should afford you enough to be able to put
  forward a piece of legislation that you yourself can write up" (numbers
  illustrative; he says "you could… make up these numbers").
- "IBDD is just a voting system… it only touches on the legislative arm." Flux the
  party also pushes transparency, "safe" enough not to "damage national security
  or commerce".

**Money** (main 00:19:06–00:21:48)

- "It gives a full ledger that shows every single vote… anonymized… But you can
  rest assured that it is one person that took that vote… as long as we stick to
  literally one rule, we vote how the app says, we can't actually be corrupted by
  the usual… briefcase full of money… if we do, everyone will know at that one
  second."
- Donors "have to manipulate the entire community… if they lie, they lose our
  trust forever, and they just blew $8 million on an advertising campaign."
- "We can't storm in and say that we're going to destroy Murdoch."
- Clive Palmer's 2019 spend: "if you remember his ads, they were idiotic… It would
  actually require, inform me why I should vote yes, because Dave on his couch
  just did a live video on YouTube for free, and it explained why I should vote
  no."
- "You can't destroy money wanting to affect politics, but you can make a system
  where it's stupidly expensive to try and lie, and you have to be transparent
  to the people."

**Defection** (main 00:21:48–00:25:14)

- The freedom of MPs to vote as they like is "actually a really nice thing" in
  normal politics: it stops a party making them "sign this contract that says
  you'll vote how I tell you". For Flux it's a weakness, but a defector "can't
  ever come back": a party defector like Cory Bernardi can say "the party has
  changed", but a Flux MP who defects is saying "I believe I'm smarter than the
  people".
- Brian reframes: defection means "I no longer believe in the system". Ben: "it
  would look shit… But it wouldn't kill flux. It wouldn't kill the idea of being
  represented by the community voting."

**Electoral path** (main 00:25:14–00:26:59)

- "I can't remember percentages… I'm going to make them up": about 4% for a
  federal Senate seat with preferences; a lower-house seat "very, very unlikely".
- WA state election (March 2021): "due to some specifics around WA election law"
  (group voting tickets in the Legislative Council) and micro-party strength,
  "maybe 2%, 2% to 3%… some, you know, strong team-ups with other
  micro-parties."
- Contacts: voteflux.org, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, LinkedIn;
  discord.io/fluxparty with "a few hundred people"; registration help wanted in
  SA, Tas, Qld, NT, and other countries.
- "It's been a long time coming, the DoD and Flux collab."

### Brian Khuu

- Frames the gap Ben concedes: "you could try to fix the way people vote, but is

## Threads

1. **Specialisation vs representativeness.** Ben's objection to juries (they
   don't reward expertise) is the mirror of Gruen's to elections (they reward a
   type). Hofkirchner (2017) splits the difference: sample, but pre-screen by
   prediction record. Three positions on one axis, all in DOD's archive.
2. **Flux admits it isn't the whole answer.** Informing voters, and the cultural
   shift away from team voting, are "the trillion dollar question". The Primer
   ten days later is DOD's attempt to map the rest.
3. **Binding the representative.** Ben (a public ledger makes defection visible
   and fatal) and Hofkirchner (a written oath) both answer "how do you make the
   MP obey the platform?", and both concede it's ultimately a moral commitment.
4. **Money in politics.** "Stupidly expensive to try and lie" is a distinct theory
   of change from Gruen's (a jury has no incentive to favour the well-funded):
   Flux accepts the money and raises its price.
5. **Trust in technology.** "You can't prove us wrong" vs the Trust episode's
   "trust but verify". A future post on voting tech could put them side by side.
