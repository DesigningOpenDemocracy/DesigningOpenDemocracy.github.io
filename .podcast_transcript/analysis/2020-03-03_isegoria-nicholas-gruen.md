## Gruen's argument, point by point

Quotes are verbatim from the **automated** transcript (fillers dropped, `…`
marks cuts). Check against the audio before quoting in DOD's own voice.

**The problem**

- "I've seen politics up close for about 30 years. And I'm pretty concerned…
  with how shallow and what I call fast foodified and divisive and polarizing…
  democratic politics is at the moment. And I think there is a big and simple
  thing that we can do" (00:00:52).

**The proposal**

- Juries represent the public differently: "we don't really think of any
  individual as a representative of the public, but we think of the jury,
  particularly when it can come to consensus… It's more considered. The
  psychology of it's different, because people aren't trying to beat each other"
  (00:01:45).
- "I would like in 10 years, 20 years time for there to be a House of Parliament,
  whether it replaces the Senate or is in addition to the Senate, which represents
  the Australian people in the way the jury represents the Australian people"
  (00:02:48).
- "While… the Houses of Parliament tell us what the parties want and what their
  backers want, while opinion polls tell us what the opinion of the people is…
  a Standing Citizens' Assembly, would show us what the considered opinion of the
  Australian people is" (00:03:39).
- "It isn't something that we have to go and get permission from anyone to start.
  We can raise the money ourselves and start doing it now" (00:04:35).
- He gave his personal email on air for a mailing list. It's already in the post's
  original notes; there's no reason to repeat it anywhere new.

**Isegoria** (00:06:20–00:14:25)

- *Parrhesia* "is often translated as freedom of speech. In fact, it means the duty
  to speak truth to power." *Isegoria* "means equality of speech… our lack of a word
  for it, our lack of a concept of it means that we don't think about it very much."
  "My wife is Greek, but then she's modern Greek."
- "Something like 95% of members of parliament are highly educated… That means that
  the whole way our political discourse is conducted… alienates… something like 40,
  50% of our population."
- **Political correctness** (00:09:18–00:10:59). "In the last 10, 15 years, there has
  been a huge ramping up of political correctness. And therefore, certain ways of
  speaking are simply proscribed." On immigration: "I don't say this [as] someone
  who is all that sympathetic to that concern. I am sympathetic to it if people care
  about it. This is supposed to be a democracy." Those people are left feeling "the
  way they think is actually being actively suppressed and attacked." **An
  isegoria argument made through a contested example; a post should keep his
  framing (equal voice, not endorsement) or choose a different one.**
- "Here's a shocking thought, you can have a democracy without elections. Now, I'm
  not actually proposing a democracy without elections."
- Athens: the Ecclesia met monthly (citizens only, "didn't include slaves… women…
  foreigners. That's obviously not something I'm saying is a good idea"); the Boule
  was "500 people, citizens chosen at random and rotated every year… that was the
  body that ran the city… and was very important in setting up the agenda." "You
  don't have to win an election to be a part of any of this. If you want to win an
  election, you have to… be articulate… well-educated… have some money or have some
  friends with money."

**Opinion vs considered opinion** (00:15:18–00:20:55)

- "We purchase that legitimacy at the expense of equality of speech. There is a
  certain kind of person who overwhelmingly wins that contest. The alternative we
  have in our courts of law… not just that the court found this person guilty, but
  that the community found them guilty."
- "If 12 ordinary people chosen at random all say, or say 10 out of the 12… that
  produces another way to determine what the people's view is… not by way of a
  competition… but by simply sampling."
- Carbon pricing: Abbott "knew that if a radio interviewer wanted to talk about it,
  he'd have to talk about it for a minute or two… A jury… would have said… We've
  looked at your policy, and it doesn't really make any sense… It simply isn't a
  policy."
- "Less about a media circus, and more about groups of people trying to figure out
  what the right answer is, and not really whose side they're on."

**What elections can't touch** (00:20:55–00:25:33)

- "Small target strategy": both majors start near 40% and avoid giving offence.
  "There are lots of areas where we've got a rough idea what to do… gun control…
  mass incarceration… drug problems… as a medical issue… politicians are just scared
  to do what the people who are experts in the field think ought to be done."
- Brian raises Bloomberg's stop-and-frisk. Gruen: a jury hearing from the black
  community might have reacted well, "I may be wrong. Certainly in the South in the
  1950s and 60s, white juries used to acquit white people of raping black women… If
  there's enough vicious racism around, then democracy works as you would expect."
  A fair admission of the mechanism's limit.

**Flux, MiVote, direct democracy** (00:25:33–00:33:56)

- "I wish them well." Flux "will need to adopt some of the kinds of compromises that
  candidates do. So they'll have to explain themselves in under eight seconds…
  highly emotive… not because they're bad people, but because they're trying to win
  elections."
- "We're every bit as much of the problem of politics as the politicians are. And a
  lot of… people saying they're going to fix democracy, are suckers for a view that
  we're the good guys and the politicians are the bad guys… They act the way they do
  because we reward them."
- "We complain about politicians' scare campaigns. And why do you think they run
  scare campaigns?… Because scare campaigns work… if we have a direct vote on a
  carbon tax or franking credits… the political system will seek to manipulate
  people." A chamber of "a hundred people… chosen at random… [who] call experts…
  and commission reports" has "a chance to stand against the circus."
- "By making the democracy more direct, I think [it] is quite likely not to solve
  the problem and might make things worse."
- MiVote: "They've come to similar conclusions to me about what the problems are…
  they're pretty keen on the idea of… a technological solution… I'm the less
  optimistic." "Intuitively, I don't like the idea of packaging up… our political
  system will make decisions on thousands of very specific things… I want a
  political system that I can trust to work stuff out on its own, rather than me
  telling it what the conclusion… should [be]."
- Jury splits: "7-5, no one cares very much… 10-2… strong evidence."

**The road-rage effect** (00:33:56–00:38:16)

- Road rage turns to shame when the other driver is "my next door neighbor". "I think
  of elections and the way we cover media as the politics of road rage." Alan Jones,
  Rush Limbaugh: "Anyone who doesn't agree with them is a fool."
- The juror's arc: the letter ("oh, gee, that's exciting"), dread ("oh god, this is
  politics… everyone's going to yell at me"), then the room: "nobody's like that…
  These people are just like me… the other people who don't like politics… they've
  tuned out of this Punch and Judy show. And they get right into it."
- "It makes manifest in the physical situation… the real nature of politics, which is
  that you can make it about you or you can make it about them. But most people would
  prefer to make it about both."
- Machiavelli: "it's only the oligarchs… who want more and more power. The ordinary
  people… want our government to work properly, we want to be left alone… and we
  don't want to pick fights."

**Scale, trolls and technology** (00:39:09–00:46:44)

- On online deliberation for everyone: "we haven't yet mastered the art of stopping
  trolls driving everybody crazy." On small samples: "60, 65 percent of a group of 150
  randomly selected Australians… that's good evidence… If it was 51 percent, that's
  not the case."
- "Vast amounts of commercial money have gone into Facebook and Twitter, and they're
  not only not trying to solve this problem, they're probably making the problem
  worse." His 2014 Club Troppo post on online tools that "amp down the aggro" (linked
  in the blog post's notes).
- Peter Pomerantsev's *This Is Not Propaganda*: "trolls… undermine people's ability to
  know what's real and what's not real, and who's saying what and why." "Facebook's
  comments can't be taken seriously. They're basically just trying to make money."
- "A Citizen Jury does… a lot of things to make it hard to infiltrate by the trolls.
  You know who these people are, they're on the electoral rolls… If people turn up with
  a conspiracy theory… the group can say, let's get some research done on that."

**Accountability** (00:47:37–00:49:20)

- "One of the beauties of a jury is that the accountability to the people is being of
  the people, not in reporting back to the people… You are sovereign and you vote…
  No one turns up and says justify why you voted." Jurors are accountable to their
  conscience and the law. "Accountability is a nice name, but like transparency, it's
  got its limitations. And it always produces its own perversities."

**The record** (00:49:20–00:53:56)

- "Something like 14 citizen juries in Australia. Virtually all of them have been very
  successful." The SA nuclear jury "didn't give the government what it wanted… It
  wasn't a brilliantly run citizen jury, and it produced some frustration."
- "When they've been easy to implement, governments have implemented them, and when
  they've not been easy to implement, guess what happens." Commissioned juries pick
  "tame" issues like Adelaide's nightlife; "they wouldn't do it on tax… some of the
  best uses of a citizen jury would be on those kinds of things, precisely for that
  reason."
- Oregon's Citizens' Initiative Review: 24 jurors, four days, 300 words for and
  against with the vote count. East Belgium (he says "two provinces… a council, I
  think it's of 50"). Madrid's sampled council. The French and UK climate assemblies.

**The standing chamber as activism** (00:53:56–00:59:31)

- "We don't have to go to the political system and ask for permission… If we can get
  funding… crowd fund… philanthropic sources… a standing citizens chamber… in which we
  see the political business of the nation hashed over by ordinary people."
- On carbon pricing: "my very strong hunch is they wouldn't have been impressed."
- "Privately funded, and it has no official standing, but it would have a lot of
  political standing." Examples: bushfire readiness funding before the 2019–20 fires;
  Adani ("maybe people in central Queensland would have a different view… and that's
  good. We need to know that").
- On the Coalition and coal: "they're funded by the coal lobby… and… they've turned it
  into a kind of culture war thing… this is sort of true on both sides." "I should make
  some unpleasant comments about the Labour Party… for balance, I'm not a huge fan."
  **Partisan-sensitive; attribute and balance, or leave out.**
- The electoral roll "is [a] public document". Brian likens it to Roy Morgan; Gruen:
  "It's a form of polling, but it's deliberative polling" (Fishkin).

**Polls, fast food and battle-hardened language** (01:00:23–01:09:15)

- "An opinion poll… record[s] the opinion of the people, but you have no idea how
  well-informed it is… whether in a room of 10 people, half of whom disagreed with them
  and came up with good arguments, whether they change." Abortion is different: views
  there rest on values, not information.
- Focus groups: "What's your impression of Australia?… 'rugged individualism and
  popping a prawn on the barbie'… And you'll go away and make an ad out of that."
- "Fast foodification of politics… instant gratification, nice stuff that tastes
  yummy… and stuff that's actually not very good for you."
- A deliberately "unacceptable" example about advising a 14-year-old on clothing,
  to show how any nuance gets "made mincemeat of". **Don't quote this; the point
  (opponents search for lines to misrepresent) stands without it.**
- Both parties run war rooms that "pore over all the speeches… They're trolls of a
  sort." So politicians use "battle-hardened language… without color, without much
  commitment… it's fast food language."
- Thatcher's "no such thing as society" and Kellyanne Conway's "alternative facts" as
  misrepresented phrases. (**Handling: omit** his characterisation of Conway, which
  is personal; his reading of the phrase can stand.)

**Funding and neutrality** (01:09:15–01:15:05)

- "I would like to do this as a form of activism. So it's not a pilot project or a
  demonstration project." "About 90… or 95 percent of them say this was really good,
  and about half of those think it's just life-changingly good." Aristotle: "a system
  in which people take turns in governing and being governed… that isn't the system we
  have."
- Trying to reach whoever manages Jeff Bezos's $10bn climate pledge; one idea is
  juries in Australia and the US ("tremendous laggards on climate change") sending a
  delegation to the Glasgow climate conference in November.
- On climate groups (Brian mentions Coalition of Everyone): "most people who are working
  in this area are activists themselves, and they're trying to bring about a particular
  result… that's not what I'm talking about… Setting this body up is not setting it up
  to come to any conclusion."
- Brexit juries: he raised money for them, from anti-Brexit donors, "because why would
  you give money to it if it wasn't?" But "if it represented the considered opinion of
  the British people, I'd think good on them… there's much more to life than an
  economy." What happened instead: "clever campaigning, some trolling, some fast food
  politics… That's no way to build political legitimacy."
- Predictions: a majority of "probably 55%" of Britons want to rejoin; "if the EU
  survives, Britain will be back within 20 years."

**The question matters** (01:15:05–01:18:38)

- Brian's "state philosopher": "Completely stupid idea as far as I'm concerned." But
  the Brexit question "pitted a completely non-specific option… with a very specific
  option". Howard, in 1999, insisted on a concrete republic model: "John Howard was
  trying to gerrymander the system in his own favor, but at least it put Australians in
  a position where they got asked… the Spice Girls question… what you really, really
  want." (He reuses the Spice Girls line at the 2026 IDoD panel.) "There is no perfect
  way through that three-cornered contest", which is a social-choice point.

## Checking the blog post against the transcript

1. **"The accountability of a jury is being *of* the people, not reporting back *to*
   the people."** Transcript: "the accountability to the people is being of the
   people, not in reporting back to the people." Close enough, but if it's in quote
   marks, use the transcript wording.
2. **The post's Oregon/Belgium summary** says "a permanent randomly selected citizens'
   council in the German-speaking community of Belgium". That's the correct
   institution. Gruen himself says "two provinces" and "50"; the post has quietly
   corrected him, which is fine, but a fact-check note would help.
3. The post leaves out the political-correctness and coal passages and the Brexit
   prediction. Reasonable choices for a post; kept here.
4. The post's "Original episode notes" name the Proportional Representation Society
   among organisations referenced. In the transcript it's only Brian's slip ("our
   current system of proportional representation"), which Gruen corrects.

## Threads

1. **Isegoria is the organising idea**, and this is the talk where DOD got the word
   properly (it dropped out of the 2017 transcript). The [isegoria concept page](../../docs/concepts/isegoria.md)
   should cite this episode, if it doesn't already.
2. **"We're part of the problem"** appears in all three Gruen recordings. It's his
   answer to every direct-democracy proposal: the voters who reward scare campaigns
   would vote in the referendums too.
3. **Neutral process vs activist cause.** He insists the chamber must not be set up to
   reach a conclusion, yet the money comes from people with causes (anti-Brexit donors,
   climate funders). He's open about the tension; a post on standing assemblies should
   be too.
4. **Juries resist trolls; mass online deliberation doesn't yet.** The live version of
   the Habermas Machine debate (DOD's [Habermas Machine post](../../docs/blog/posts/2026-08-16-habermas-machine-ai-mediation.md)).
5. **The question design problem** (Brexit vs the 1999 republic) connects to
   Hofkirchner's Greek referendum in 2017 and Ben Ballingall's "who wrote the
   question?" in February 2020. Three DOD guests independently landed on it.
