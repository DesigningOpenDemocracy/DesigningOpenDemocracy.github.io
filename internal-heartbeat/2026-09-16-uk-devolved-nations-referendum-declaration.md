# Scotland/Wales/NI joint independence declaration — research note, not yet public

**Status (2026-09-16):** Research lead surfaced this run. Not promoted to
the public September sync post — see reasoning below.

## What's happening

On Monday, 14 September 2026, in Cardiff, the leaders of the three parties
currently governing Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland's devolved
administrations — SNP leader John Swinney, Plaid Cymru leader Rhun ap
Iorwerth, and Sinn Féin's Michelle O'Neill (joined by Sinn Féin president
Mary Lou McDonald) — signed a joint memorandum asserting their nations'
right to "self-determination," stating "No Westminster government has the
right to block democracy or undermine the principle that our people will
decide their own future," and calling for referendum processes in each
nation (independence for Scotland and Wales, unification with the
Republic of Ireland for Northern Ireland).[^ajz][^irt] It is reported as
the first time all three devolved nations have simultaneously been led by
parties supporting leaving the UK.[^irt] UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham
has said a Scottish referendum is "off limits" and a Northern Ireland
unification referendum "off the table."[^ajz] Northern Ireland's Deputy
First Minister Emma Little-Pengelly (DUP) accused O'Neill of trying to
"weaponise the role of First Minister" by attending without her
agreement.[^irt]

## Why this is a live governance-design story, not just a partisan headline

The framework-relevant angle isn't which side of the independence question
is right — it's a structural question about who controls access to a
constitutional referendum mechanism: three sub-national governments are
asserting a right to trigger self-determination votes that the central
government they're part of says isn't theirs to grant. That's a genuine
federalism/devolution accountability question — whose "yes" is required
before a democratic mechanism (a referendum) can even be offered to
voters — independent of how anyone feels about the underlying
independence causes.

## Why this isn't in the public draft this run

Highly politically loaded in a way that needs care to write about
responsibly in one pass: multiple live nationalist/unionist movements,
direct party-political actors (SNP, Plaid Cymru, Sinn Féin, DUP), a
contested internal NI power-sharing dispute (Little-Pengelly's objection)
layered on top of the UK-wide question, and a sitting government's
position that could shift quickly. Two days old, still developing (a
declaration and a call for a process, not an event that has concluded).
Similar shape to the Saxony-Anhalt AfD item parked 2026-09-14 — worth
watching rather than writing up under this run's time budget.

## What would make this ready

- Whether the "first-in-history" framing about all three devolved nations
  being simultaneously led by pro-referendum parties holds up against a
  neutral secondary source (not just the two wire pieces cited here).
- Whether Westminster's position moves in either direction, or whether a
  legal/constitutional process (a Section 30 order equivalent, a
  Belfast/Good Friday Agreement poll trigger) actually gets invoked in any
  of the three cases.
- If drafted, the framing should stay on the structural
  who-controls-the-mechanism question per the framework's Voice guidance,
  not narrate the merits of any nation's independence case.
