## What it argues (summary, our words)

A reading of the city-builder game *Frostpunk 2* (its sandbox "Utopia
Builder" mode, before the Fractured Utopias update) as a political
philosophy argument made through game mechanics rather than story — what
game theorist Ian Bogost calls *procedural rhetoric*. The hook: a player
can end up running an authoritarian city without ever deciding to, through a
long chain of individually reasonable crisis decisions.

The essay maps the game's systems onto four thinkers:

- **Hegel (zeitgeist, dialectic).** The city's ideological drift across
  three zero-sum axes (progress/adaptation, merit/equality,
  reason/tradition) produces a governing faction, and then an opposition
  faction made of exactly the people that drift left out. Synthesis is
  never guaranteed because the axes are built as trade-offs.
- **Carl Schmitt vs. Chantal Mouffe (antagonism vs. agonism).** Tension never
  fully resolves (Schmitt: conflict is the baseline condition); faction
  "fervor" escalates disagreement from negotiation to refusal to violence
  (friend/enemy). The council-vote bargaining is presented as Mouffe's
  agonism made playable: adversaries who still recognise each other's
  legitimacy, a settlement that has to be actively rebuilt every session.
  The essay is explicit that Schmitt was a Nazi jurist and doesn't sanitise
  that.
- **Hannah Arendt (the social question).** Early survival play is pure
  necessity, with one defensible answer per problem and no room for
  politics; citizens appear only as aggregate numbers. Each new crisis lets
  necessity crowd deliberation back out.
- **Schmitt + Arendt on emergency rule.** "Sovereign is he who decides on
  the exception": in a permanent frost, the emergency argument is always
  available. The game's escalating "rule law" tree (militia, martial law,
  propaganda, secret police, finally dissolving the council) needs a
  supermajority but passes more easily under high tension, so the council
  votes its own powers away. Arendt's "banality of evil" is used for the
  structural point: the interface pushes the player to think in metrics
  rather than people.

Closing claim: the game's argument is that democratic governance is the
exception, not the default, and authoritarianism is the path of least
resistance when a permanent emergency meets tools that frame people as
problems — and that a game, unlike a book, makes *you* the one doing it.

## Why it's relevant to DOD

- Accessible on-ramp to agonistic democracy, emergency powers and democratic
  backsliding for an audience that wouldn't pick up Mouffe or Arendt.
- The "erosion through a hundred reasonable decisions" framing is directly
  about governance design: what institutional guardrails stop emergency
  powers ratcheting? That's a question DOD's landscape cares about.
- Games as a medium for civic/deliberative education — possibly worth a
  broader lead of its own (other civic simulation games) if more turn up.

## Notes for whoever picks this up

- Game-mechanics details (supermajority thresholds, faction timing, law
  names) come from the essay, not verified against the game; don't repeat
  them as fact under DOD's name without checking.
- The essay's philosophy is a popular-level reading; it's explicit about
  simplifying Hegel. Fine for a share, worth framing as "one reading" if
  used anywhere more permanent.
- Nonpartisanship: the essay isn't partisan, but its "authoritarianism is the
  default" thesis is an opinion — attribute it to the essay, not to DOD.
