# Sierra Leone's constitutional-reform vote-count dispute — research note, not yet public

**Status (2026-09-28):** Research lead. Not promoted to the public draft
this pass — see reasoning below. Revisit once the Supreme Court has actually
ruled (due 7 October 2026), likely the natural pick for October's sync post
rather than a late addition squeezed into an already-full September one.

## What's happening

Sierra Leone's parliament passed a package of constitutional amendments
covering the 2028 election rules: lowering the presidential-election
threshold from 55% to 50%+1, making proportional representation permanent
for parliamentary elections, a 30% minimum for women candidates, and
allowing dual nationals to stand for parliament/hold certain offices.

The dispute is specifically about whether the bill actually cleared the
constitutional bar to pass it. Section 108 of the 1991 constitution
requires a two-thirds majority — at least 100 of 149 MPs. On 10 August
2026 parliament voted 96–41 on a key clause (concerning the electoral
commission); the opposition All People's Congress then walked out with 49
MPs mid-session. The Speaker ruled that the two-thirds threshold applies to
members "present and voting," not all 149 seats — which let the bill pass
despite the raw count (96) falling short of 100.

Rather than sign the amendment, President Julius Maada Bio referred the
counting-method question to the Supreme Court on 14 September 2026, saying
the government wants "constitutional clarity" first; he says he'll announce
whether he signs only after the Court rules. The ruling is due 7 October
2026.[^riotimes]

## Why this is a live governance-design story, not just Sierra Leone politics

This is structurally close kin to two of this month's already-published
items: like Missouri's referendum fight, it's a test of whether a
constitution's own supermajority threshold — a hard, textual check on
constitutional change — can be satisfied by a procedural reading (count only
those present, not the whole chamber) that an opposition walkout can be used
to trigger. Unlike Missouri, though, the dispute here isn't between two
branches disagreeing about which mechanism binds; it's about whether the
first branch (parliament) actually cleared its own bar at all, with the
question now properly in front of the one body positioned to answer it
(the Supreme Court) before the president will act either way. That's the
part worth naming if it holds up: the referral itself is a working example
of a check functioning as designed, not (yet) evidence of a check being
routed around.

The complicating wrinkle, worth being honest about before writing this up
publicly: critics note the amendment package (especially the lowered
presidential threshold) plausibly favours Bio's own SLPP in 2028, even
though term limits bar Bio himself from running again — so his own delay
plausibly serves multiple purposes (genuine institutional caution,
managing internal SLPP succession politics, or just avoiding ownership of
a court fight over his party's own bill) that aren't possible to
disentangle from this distance without more reporting.
