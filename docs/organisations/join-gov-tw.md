Join (join.gov.tw, officially the Public Policy Online Participation Platform / 公共政策網路參與平臺) is a government-run civic participation platform operated by Taiwan's National Development Council, launched in 2015 with assistance from Digital Minister Audrey Tang's office.[^amcham] It grew out of the same 2014 Sunflower Movement moment that produced [vTaiwan](vtaiwan.md) and [g0v](g0v.md), but took a different institutional path: rather than a volunteer civic-tech collaboration, Join was built and is run directly by government, giving its recommendations formal procedural weight that vTaiwan's advisory-only model never had.

## How it works

Join centres on a **petition mechanism**: any citizen can submit a policy proposal, and if it gathers **5,000 supporters within 60 days**, the relevant government agency is legally required to provide a reasoned public response.[^bertelsmann] Since January 2016 the Executive Yuan has also required agencies under its jurisdiction to post proposed regulations to Join for a 60-day public comment period before finalising them.[^amcham] The platform also runs open consultations ("眾开講"), legislative monitoring, participatory budgeting, and a channel to contact officials directly.

By June 2022, Join had received 13,853 total proposals, of which 289 reached the 5,000-signature threshold requiring a government response.[^bertelsmann]

## Comparison with vTaiwan

Analysts studying both platforms describe a genuine division of labour rather than redundancy: Join is "a clear and well-designed platform, making it easy to petition and rally support," while vTaiwan's Pol.is-based deliberation is more tech-intensive and better suited to surfacing consensus on contested, multi-sided questions.[^bertelsmann] Join's noted weaknesses are the mirror image of vTaiwan's strengths — "little in-depth interaction in the petitioning process," and a relatively low signature threshold that risks a non-diverse pool of supporters driving government attention.[^bertelsmann] Join's strength is institutionalisation: formal procedural backing and dedicated participation officers inside government, rather than a volunteer community's continued goodwill.

Join also reaches further than vTaiwan ever did — an older, less tech-savvy public, and policy areas well outside vTaiwan's digital/tech remit, including drunk-driving law, sexual assault legislation, and child-abuse policy.[^vtaiwan-blog] In effect, vTaiwan proved the model was viable and the state absorbed it: a win for the underlying practice of digital public consultation in Taiwan, even as it left vTaiwan itself without the international spotlight role it held in the platform's early years.
