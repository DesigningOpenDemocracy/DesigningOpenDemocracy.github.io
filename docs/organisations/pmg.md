The Parliamentary Monitoring Group is an information service in Cape Town that does one thing, and has done it since 1995: send a monitor into every open committee meeting of South Africa's Parliament, record it, minute it, and publish the result.

It exists because of a specific gap. South Africa's parliamentary committees are where legislation is actually negotiated, departments are questioned, and oversight either happens or doesn't — and there was no official public record of any of it. PMG was set up as a joint project of three anti-apartheid-era advocacy organisations — [Black Sash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sash), the Human Rights Committee, and [Idasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Democracy_in_South_Africa) — to produce "a type of Hansard" for committee proceedings so those organisations could lobby Parliament on the basis of what had actually been said.[^pmg-founded] The website followed in 1998 and turned an internal service for three NGOs into a public archive.[^pmg-web] PMG became an independent NGO in its own right in July 2009.[^pmg-ngo]

## What it publishes

Its core output is a written report of each committee meeting, published within three working days along with every document tabled — public submissions, working drafts of bills, and departmental briefings. The audio recording goes up immediately after the meeting, before the written report is ready.[^pmg-what] Around that sit a bill tracker, ministerial replies to written questions, early notice of calls for public comment and public hearings, attendance records, and research on how Parliament itself functions.

Coverage is close to total: every committee except Intelligence, which is closed to the public, with the archive running from January 1998 to the present.[^pmg-coverage]

## The funding model, and a recent change

PMG's free public access is cross-subsidised — commercial and government subscribers pay, which keeps the service open for civil society.[^pmg-funding] That structure had a visible cost: some material sat behind a subscription wall. As of 2026 the site carries a standing notice that this has been rolled back for the historical archive: "Premium content from before 2025 is now available for everyone!"[^pmg-premium]

This is the kind of change worth naming plainly. An accountability record that is technically public but priced out of reach of the people it documents is a weaker instrument than it looks; opening three decades of committee proceedings closes that gap for everything except the current year.

## Why it belongs in the landscape

PMG is one of the longest-running parliamentary monitoring organisations anywhere, and unusually it predates the civic-tech wave that produced most of its counterparts — [mySociety](mysociety.md) in the UK, the [OpenAustralia Foundation](open-australia-foundation.md) and [They Vote For You](they-vote-for-you.md) in Australia, [Mzalendo](mzalendo.md) in Kenya. Where those started from "the data exists, let's build a site," PMG started from "the record does not exist, so we will create it" — a labour-intensive human monitoring operation rather than a scraping problem. It still is one: PMG openly recruits monitors to write up committee meetings.[^pmg-recruit]

Its sister site, [People's Assembly](https://www.pa.org.za/), covers representatives at national, provincial and local level.
