## What happened

Between 2019 and 2020, Victoria's Independent Broad-based Anti-corruption Commission (IBAC) ran **Operation Sandon**, a public investigation into corruption allegations involving City of Casey councillors, property developer John Woodman, and his associates.[^ibac-overview]

IBAC's special report, tabled in the Victorian Parliament in 2023, named councillors **Sam Aziz** and **Geoff Ablett** as having received substantial personal benefits from Woodman in exchange for supporting his planning and rezoning interests in Cranbourne West.[^ibac-findings] The report also described a wider pattern of orchestrated influence over council decision-making.[^ibac-quote] Separately, IBAC found that Casey councillors as a group exhibited and tolerated conduct that fell below the standard required of them — a finding about the council's collective culture, distinct from the individual findings against Aziz and Ablett.

## Fallout

- Two councillors resigned in mid-February 2020, ahead of the council's expected dismissal.[^resignations]
- The Victorian Government passed legislation to sack the entire council, after an independent monitor's report by Laurinda Gardner recommended dismissal over the corruption allegations linked to Woodman.[^monitor-dismissal]
- Administrators were installed to run Casey; contemporary reporting at the time of the dismissal (February 2020) described administrators potentially running the council for up to five years, until the following scheduled general election.[^monitor-dismissal]
- In September 2025, IBAC announced that a former Casey councillor and a property development consultant had been charged with offences arising from Operation Sandon — including receiving secret commissions, misconduct in public office, and misuse of position for the former councillor, and giving secret commissions for the consultant. **IBAC did not publicly name either person charged, and said it would not comment further while the matter was before the courts.**[^charges] Some news reporting associated the charges with Aziz and Ablett by name, but this note could not independently confirm that identification against IBAC's own (deliberately unnamed) announcement — **do not treat the named individuals from the 2023 report as confirmed to be the same people facing the 2025 criminal charges** without checking current court records.

## Wider significance

IBAC's investigation examined broader systemic issues affecting the integrity of Victoria's planning process, including the operation of donation laws and how the conduct evidenced in the investigation could be prevented in future.[^ibac-systemic]

## Framing note for DOD

It is tempting to read local-government corruption risk as a story about an unaccountable, unelected bureaucrat — the CEO — exploiting a lack of citizen oversight. Casey is the opposite of that story: **the point of failure IBAC identified was the elected layer** (individual councillors, and the council's collective tolerance of substandard conduct), not the CEO or administrative arm. See [How Victorian Councils Are Governed](how-victorian-councils-are-governed.md) for the formal split between the two.

Casey is also, in one specific sense, the *inverse* of a true [accountability sink](../concepts/accountability-sink.md): the feedback loop here functioned. IBAC's investigation named specific individuals in its 2023 report, Parliament acted on the municipal monitor's findings, and (per the September 2025 charges, still before the courts and against people IBAC itself declined to name) potential legal consequences may follow. In a genuine accountability sink, responsibility is structurally unattributable — no one specific can be identified or held to account. Casey shows a system where, however slowly and after however much damage, responsibility *was* eventually traced back to named findings and named institutional failures. That is a useful contrast case, not a reason for complacency about how long it took or what it cost residents in the meantime.
