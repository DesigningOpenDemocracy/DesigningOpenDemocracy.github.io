## What happened

Between 2019 and 2020, Victoria's Independent Broad-based Anti-corruption Commission (IBAC) ran **Operation Sandon**, a public investigation into corruption allegations involving City of Casey councillors, property developer John Woodman, and his associates.[^ibac-overview]

IBAC's special report, tabled in the Victorian Parliament in 2023, found that councillors **Sam Aziz** and **Geoff Ablett** accepted substantial personal benefits from Woodman in exchange for supporting his planning and rezoning interests in Cranbourne West, and repeatedly failed to declare their conflicts of interest in doing so.[^ibac-findings] Separately, IBAC found that Casey councillors **as a group** exhibited and tolerated conduct that fell below the standard required of them — a finding about the council's collective culture, distinct from the individual findings against Aziz and Ablett. Other councillors were not found to have received a direct personal benefit from Woodman.[^ibac-findings]

## Fallout

- A 2019/2020 report by municipal monitor Laurinda Gardner found clear evidence that the council had failed to provide good governance, and recommended its dismissal.[^monitor-dismissal]
- Two councillors resigned before the dismissal.[^monitor-dismissal]
- On 19 February 2020, the Victorian Parliament passed the *Local Government (Casey City Council) Act 2020*, dismissing the entire council and appointing administrators to run it in residents' and ratepayers' interests.[^monitor-dismissal]
- Administrators governed Casey until the deferred election on the fourth Saturday of October 2024.[^monitor-dismissal]
- In September 2025, IBAC announced criminal charges against Aziz and Ablett arising from Operation Sandon, with a first court date set at the Melbourne Magistrates' Court.[^charges] **As of this note's most recent check, no outcome of that court process has been confirmed** — treat the charges as allegations before the court, not an established finding, and verify current status before citing this further.

## Wider significance

IBAC's public report went beyond Casey specifically: it identified systemic corruption vulnerabilities in how planning-scheme amendment decisions are made, at both the local and state government level — including that safeguards around amending planning schemes had been bypassed, and that amendments reached decision-makers without clear strategic justification. IBAC made 34 recommendations aimed at closing those vulnerabilities across the sector, not just at Casey.[^ibac-systemic]

## Framing note for DOD

It is tempting to read local-government corruption risk as a story about an unaccountable, unelected bureaucrat — the CEO — exploiting a lack of citizen oversight. Casey is the opposite of that story: **the point of failure IBAC identified was the elected layer** (individual councillors, and the council's collective tolerance of substandard conduct), not the CEO or administrative arm. See [How Victorian Councils Are Governed](how-victorian-councils-are-governed.md) for the formal split between the two.

Casey is also, in one specific sense, the *inverse* of a true [accountability sink](../concepts/accountability-sink.md): the feedback loop here functioned. IBAC's investigation named specific individuals, Parliament acted on the municipal monitor's findings, and (per the September 2025 charges, still before the courts) potential legal consequences followed. In a genuine accountability sink, responsibility is structurally unattributable — no one specific can be identified or held to account. Casey shows a system where, however slowly and after however much damage, responsibility *was* eventually traced back to named individuals and named institutional failures. That is a useful contrast case, not a reason for complacency about how long it took or what it cost residents in the meantime.
