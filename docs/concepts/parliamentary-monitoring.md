title: Parliamentary Monitoring
summary: "Independent, systematic observation of a legislature — recording what representatives say, how they vote, which committees they sit on and what they spend — and publishing it in a form ordinary citizens can actually use."
---

**Parliamentary monitoring** is the practice of independently and systematically watching a legislature and publishing what it does. The organisations that do it are usually called **parliamentary monitoring organisations (PMOs)**. When the sector wrote down its own principles in 2012, more than 90 of them across over 60 countries signed on.[^dpo]

The premise is narrow and worth stating plainly: in most democracies the parliamentary record is already *public*. Hansard exists. Committee meetings are open. Voting is recorded. And almost none of it is *usable* — it is scattered across PDFs, unindexed, unattributed, published weeks late, or (for committee work, where most legislation is actually shaped) simply never written down at all. A record that technically exists but that no citizen can realistically search is functionally a secret.

PMOs close that gap from the outside, without waiting for the institution to reform itself.

## What the work actually involves

Different PMOs sit at different points on a spectrum:

- **Reformatting an existing record** — scraping official Hansard and voting data and turning it into per-representative profiles, plain-English vote summaries, and searchable archives. [TheyWorkForYou](../organisations/mysociety.md) and [They Vote For You](../organisations/they-vote-for-you.md) are of this type.
- **Creating a record that does not exist** — sending human monitors into committee rooms to minute proceedings that no official transcript covers. [South Africa's Parliamentary Monitoring Group](../organisations/pmg.md) has done this continuously since 1995.
- **Analysing and scoring** — annual scorecards on a sitting parliament's performance, attendance records, and research on legislative openness, as [Mzalendo](../organisations/mzalendo.md) does in Kenya and [PRS Legislative Research](../organisations/prs-legislative-research.md) in India.
- **Tracking money rather than words** — the fiscal analogue: following an allocation from a budget line through to whether the thing was built. [BudgIT](../organisations/budgit.md)'s Tracka is the clearest example.

Most established PMOs do several of these at once, and increasingly they also run the return path — structured channels for citizens to submit views on bills, which official public-participation processes tend to make deliberately hard to use.

## Why it matters for democratic design

Parliamentary monitoring is best understood as an anti-[accountability sink](accountability-sink.md) mechanism. A representative body works as an accountability chain only if the link between "how my representative voted" and "how I vote next time" is legible to the voter. Where that link is broken by sheer illegibility, representation degrades into a formality — the vote is on the record, and no one can find it.

Two things follow that are easy to miss:

**Monitoring is not neutral observation.** Deciding what to score, which votes count as significant, and how to summarise a division in plain English are editorial acts. Good PMOs are explicit about their methodology and non-partisan about their conclusions; the ones worth trusting publish how they decided, not just what they decided.

**The constitutional hook matters.** Where legislative openness is a constitutional obligation rather than a norm — Kenya's Article 118(1) requires Parliament to "facilitate public participation and involvement" — monitoring shifts from advocacy to enforcement. The organisation is no longer asking the institution to be more open; it is documenting a failure to comply.

## Where to go next

- **The Declaration on Parliamentary Openness (2012)** — the sector's founding statement of principles, launched on the International Day of Democracy at the World e-Parliament Conference in Rome, with backing from more than 90 PMOs across over 60 countries.[^dpo] [NDI's announcement](https://www.ndi.org/declaration-parliamentary-openness) is the most reliable live account of it.
- **OpeningParliament.org — gone.** The network these organisations coordinated through was a joint initiative of NDI, the Sunlight Foundation and the Latin American Network for Legislative Transparency.[^opening] The domain has since lapsed and now resolves to an unrelated commercial gambling site, so it should not be linked. Archived copies survive in the [Wayback Machine](https://web.archive.org/web/*/openingparliament.org/*). This is worth naming rather than quietly routing around: the shared infrastructure of a transparency sector is itself subject to link rot, and a dead coordinating site is a real loss of institutional memory, not a broken bookmark.
- The [Open Government Partnership](https://www.opengovpartnership.org/)'s legislative-openness work, which is where several PMOs press their national parliaments into binding commitments
