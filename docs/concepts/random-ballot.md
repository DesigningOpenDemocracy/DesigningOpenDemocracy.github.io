# Random Ballot

A **random ballot** (also called *random dictatorship*) decides a vote by drawing one cast ballot at random and adopting whatever it chose. In Wikipedia's words, it "is a randomized electoral system where the election is decided on the basis of a single randomly selected ballot."[^rb-definition] An option backed by 30% of voters therefore wins 30% of the time.

**Not the same as [sortition](sortition.md).** Both use a lottery, but for different jobs: sortition draws *people* (a jury, a citizens' assembly), while a random ballot draws an *outcome* from the votes people have already cast.

The case for it is fairness. Allan Gibbard showed in 1977 that it is "the unique social choice rule that treats all voters equally while still being strategyproof in all situations"[^rb-gibbard]: nobody gains by voting for anything other than their true favourite. The case against is just as plain: in Gibbard's own words, such a method "leaves too much to chance",[^rb-chance] and it is "rarely, if ever, proposed as a genuine electoral system".[^rb-rarely] Its usual roles are as a tiebreaker and as a thought experiment, notably Akhil Reed Amar's 1984 *Yale Law Journal* article "Choosing representatives by lottery voting".[^rb-amar]

A DOD member has argued that it fits **repeated, everyday group decisions** better than one-off ones, and proposed it as an optional "draw by lot" result mode for [MieuxVoter](../organisations/mieuxvoter.md)'s offline [Majority Judgment](majority-judgment.md) app, Urn.[^rb-urn-issue] The argument: a winner-take-all method lets a consistent minority lose every round, while a draw weighted by support gives it a proportional share of wins over time, and a single unlucky draw matters little when the decision is small and comes round again. The proposal keeps Majority Judgment as the default, excludes from the draw any option whose median grade is "Reject", and shows the odds alongside the result.
