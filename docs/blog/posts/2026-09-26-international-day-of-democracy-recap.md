On 15 September, International Day of Democracy,[^unday] DOD and [888 Co-operative Causeway](../../organisations/888-cooperative-causeway.md) filled a room at The Kelvin Club in Melbourne for ["democracy beyond the ballot box"](2026-08-25-international-day-of-democracy-event.md). The panel had four speakers: an economist, a facilitator, a degrowth scholar and a co-op founder. The crowd was big enough that extra chairs had to be found at the last minute.

<!-- more -->

**Main lesson** —

- "Representative" doesn't have to mean "elected". Choosing people by lot, the way juries already work, is another way to represent the public, and the practitioners on the panel say it works when it's tried.
- Deliberation works because of its structure: random invitation, paid time, slow learning, and the group making its own choices. Automate the choosing and you remove the part that changes people.
- The panel agreed on the diagnosis but not on where power actually sits: in parliament, in workplaces and unions, or with capital. That's the argument worth continuing.

*Quotes below are from the event recording's transcript, which was checked against the audio. They're lightly trimmed: `…` marks a cut, and verbal fillers are dropped. The full transcript is linked under Sources.*

## The panel

[888 Co-operative Causeway](../../organisations/888-cooperative-causeway.md)'s chair, Antony McMullen, moderated. 888's patron, Basil Varghese, opened with an Acknowledgement of Country and closed the night. The panellists:

- **Nicholas Gruen**, CEO of [Lateral Economics](../../organisations/lateral-economics.md), and a long-time advocate of citizens' juries and assemblies chosen by lot
- **Nicole Hunter**, co-founder and Managing Director of [MosaicLab](../../organisations/mosaiclab.md), who led the Yarra Valley Water citizens' jury
- **Anitra Nelson**, an activist scholar at the University of Melbourne who writes on degrowth, housing and post-money economies
- **Godfrey Moase**, co-founder of the energy co-op CoPower and former Executive Director of the United Workers Union

The event was timed for the run-up to Victoria's state election on 28 November.[^vicelection]

## Where each of them started

Antony opened by asking what first drew each speaker to democracy. The four answers mapped out the rest of the evening.

Godfrey's answer was cheese pizza: a night in Berkeley in 2010 at the Cheese Board Collective, a workers' co-op.

> "I thought, you know what, this is a pretty good way of running the world. So I'm a bread and roses kind of guy, and sometimes bread can be very persuasive."
> — Godfrey Moase

For Anitra, the hook was living in a housing and working commune, "and finding that direct democracy can actually work in practice."

Nicole came from years of government engagement work that was, "to be really honest… really boring". What changed her mind was seeing how committed people became in deeper processes. One participant called in to a citizens' jury from a hospital room where their wife was having a baby. Another joined online during COVID while having dialysis.

> "That's when the little hairs on my arms go up, and I go, this we could do everywhere."
> — Nicole Hunter

Nicholas went for provocation: "if by democracy you mean the system we have today, this is the system that brought you Donald Trump and Adolf Hitler." He argued that powerful people hostile to democracy are very serious, while supporters of citizens' assemblies are "pretty modest in their appetites". He wants to "engineer some kind of collision" between the two ways of doing democracy. He noted that advisory assemblies are easy for politicians to absorb: "an issues management exercise for a few months, and that's that."

## "Is tech gonna save us?"

Antony described a 1950s handbook for committee secretaries he'd found in an old bookshop. Its foreword celebrated the new leisure time people had for committees and community life. Today, he said, the answer to everything is "there's an app for that". So can technology stand in for time we no longer have?

Nobody on the panel thought so.

- **Nicholas** questioned the premise that democracy means everyone doing everything. We already trust a democratic institution most of us never sit on: the jury. "There needs to be a division of labor." He doesn't want to attend a meeting about garbage collection. He wants to trust that the decision was "reasonably made", not watch the "complete panto" of talking points and gotcha interviews.
- **Nicole** said tech will "help and hinder", and that every method, "be it a method in a room, will exclude people and include people". She has watched a fully automated global "deliberation" run by Meta: "you get a time, you fill out a thing, you move on… it is terrifying in my eyes. Because there is no meeting of the minds."
- **Godfrey** said technology "displaces social cooperation", even though the technology only exists because of that cooperation. Reaching for an app crowds out asking what the organisational solution is. The deeper shortage is time: "we need a way of fighting for shorter working hours… so that participation is not bounded by privilege."

## Inside a citizens' jury

Nicole described how MosaicLab runs a jury, using the Yarra Valley Water process as her example:

- **Selection.** A two-stage random draw from the utility's customers, stratified by age, gender, location and other factors: "a mini version of the customers in the room". She stressed that an invitation works better than a public notice. "If you get an invitation to a party, you're more likely to go."
- **Time.** At least six days together, and half of it is learning. Participants ask questions and pick their own speakers.
- **Pay.** A minimum-wage stipend. One woman signed up for the money because she needed dental work. "Oh my god, she was the best participant I've ever had." She still advocates for that organisation today.
- **Ownership.** Participants write their own report. The utility commits to including it "to the maximum extent possible" in its price submission to the regulator.

Antony asked how people chosen by lot "learn to be free" without being steered. Nicole's answer was the most practical of the night:

- Experts are prepared in advance to "let go control". In the room they're a resource and an observer, not a participant.
- Learning builds up from pairs, to threes, to the whole group. She uses "speed dialogue… like speed dating, but better", with an expert rotating between small groups.
- Trade-offs are argued out loud, with people physically standing on one side of the room or the other.

She called the final step the "most sacred part": the group choosing and building its own recommendations.

> "That is like the turning point where they go from me to we… there are people in the world who are saying, yeah, we could get AI to do that for us, they could just theme up their ideas, and I'm like, uh-uh, don't touch it, because that choice making the group is doing at that moment has them really understand what they're saying and owning it."
> — Nicole Hunter

This is a direct counterpoint to AI tools built to draft a group's common ground for it, like the [Habermas Machine](2026-08-16-habermas-machine-ai-mediation.md) DOD wrote about last month.

## Elections as oligarchy

Godfrey made the evening's key historical point almost in passing, while talking about CoPower and money in politics:

> "If you read Aristotle's politics, elections weren't in the section on democracy. Elections were in the section on oligarchy."
> — Godfrey Moase

Nicholas built his second answer on it. Elections "separate the governed from the governing" and are "competitive by definition". Selection by lot means "people taking turns in governing and being governed, which is exactly what happens in juries." It accepts a division of labour without disturbing "the essential equality of people". His key distinction:

> "Representation by sampling rather than representation by elections, with all of the profound differences that that involves to equality, to the framing of what's going on."
> — Nicholas Gruen

He said competitive media now does to a competitive political system "what happens to a fly when you spray Mortein on it… you dissolve its nervous system."

## Structure is what makes shared power work

Antony raised Jo Freeman's 1970s essay [*The Tyranny of Structurelessness*](https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm). Freeman wrote it after realising that the apparently flat feminist collectives she belonged to still had decision-makers, just invisible ones.

Anitra agreed. "A lot of people misrepresent anarchy as being chaos… Anarchy is really self-governance. And you have to have processes and they have to be transparent and everything has to be accountable." Her commune met every Monday morning to divide up the week's work, with set rules about what counted as work.

Godfrey took the idea to authority itself: "Authority shouldn't be dependent on hierarchy." He argued the best leadership exists "on the border between systems", with people acting as translators between an ecosystem and a village, or between an enterprise and its industry.

Every speaker came at this point from a different direction: the commune rota, Freeman's warning, Nicole's heavily designed jury process, and Nicholas's reference to Robert Michels' iron law of oligarchy. Participation without designed structure doesn't stay democratic.

## A standing citizens' assembly, no permission needed

Nicholas's concrete proposal was the biggest idea of the night. Philanthropists, or the public, should fund a standing citizens' assembly of around a hundred randomly selected Australians to shadow federal parliament.

> "No, don't ask for any permission. Just run it, turn people over every six months or two years or whatever you decide."
> — Nicholas Gruen

He gave two costs, for two different scopes. At first he said he couldn't do it himself because he doesn't "have $10 million". In the Q&A he estimated "maybe two million to run a citizen assembly for six months to a year", and then fundraising on the back of that. Such an assembly would have no formal power, but it would put a different logic in public view. His example was carbon pricing. Had such a body existed in 2013, he argued, a prime minister set on abolishing it would have had "a bigger challenge on his hands than getting through a 10-minute radio interview."

Asked by an audience member whether it should start smaller, he rejected the idea outright. "It's absolutely not an idea of starting small. That is the opiate of activists everywhere." He calls his approach "radical incrementalism": one body run on a different institutional logic, dropped into the system "like a bit of ink in a bit of water". He pointed to Michigan, where a ballot initiative handed the drawing of electoral boundaries to a commission of randomly drawn citizens.[^michigan] (On the night he couldn't recall who led the campaign; it was Katie Fahey.) As for what the assembly should focus on, he'd leave that to the assembly.

## Where does power actually sit?

One audience member pushed on urgency. Democracy had produced Hitler and Trump, they said, and in Germany "two weeks ago, democracy gave us Alternative for Deutschland", as well as One Nation at home. How do you get from a fringe conversation to action? The panel's answers showed the evening's one real disagreement:

- **Nicholas**: fund the standing assembly and "organize collisions between the way we're doing politics now and another way of doing politics".
- **Godfrey**: build organisations that give people something else to do. He said that when people feel alienated, parties like One Nation and the AfD, and Trump, become "a flashing red button that they can hit". Pressing it only reinforces the existing structures: a "brilliantly designed negative feedback loop". His own push for a more radical labour-movement strategy stalled, he said, because it meant shifting power from leaders like himself to rank-and-file delegates, "an incredibly threatening proposition".
- **Anitra**: "It's a trap to think that the political realm is where… the decisions are being made. The biggest decisions… are being made by capitalists." She pointed to data centres arriving without public input. She also noted that 15 September was the fifth anniversary of AUKUS,[^aukus] and said "we're going to have to break the rules of this society in order to change things."

So is the lever parliament, the workplace, or capital? The panel didn't settle it.

## Future generations, and what we can live with

Another audience member asked whether randomly selected citizens would simply vote their own short-term interest against future generations.

- **Nicole** described processes, which she said are used a lot in Japan, where a few participants are assigned to speak for the future: "to act like you're the future, or to act like you're the trees". "I'm not saying it's perfect."
- **Anitra** said framing matters: ask a question in terms of living "a one planet lifestyle", and future generations are already built into it.
- **Nicholas** said a jury isn't a vote-counting contest. Rather than asking "who's got 51% of the votes", a jury asks "what can we live with?". He drew on Hannah Arendt: the secret ballot, while not a bad idea, "leans heavily on the idea of privacy and therefore of the individual rather than the res publica".
- **Godfrey**: "I think capitalism gives democracy a bad name." If people won't vote for a lower quality of life, he said, that's because they're scrambling to make a living in a system that is "parasitic upon our present now as well as our future". The good life isn't a ready-made meal, or "always feeling so freaking tired".

A third question, from someone who works in urban planning, asked how degrowth works in practice. Anitra described "commoning", universal basic services with limits, planning rules that set a *maximum* rather than a minimum on housing space, and rules that enable people to build and repair their own homes rather than rules written for industry.

## Closing: the frog in the well

Basil closed with a childhood story from Kerala. When he was six, his grandfather took him to the family well and asked him to look properly. At the bottom was a little green frog. His grandfather said it was very happy there, but that Basil's task in life was "to climb out of the well… and that frog will see the whole sky."

> "Tonight we have had four people coming out of their wells and creating and croaking, and now hopefully creating great song."
> — Basil Varghese

He ended on where he thinks democracy really lives: "when people want to live their lives in good company, reasonably comfortable, and enjoy the good life."

## Relevance to DOD

Several threads here reach back years in DOD's own archive. Nicholas made the case for citizens' juries at a DOD event back in [2017](2017-08-21-podcast.md). In [*Isegoria*](2020-03-20.md) in 2020, he argued that elections destroy the equal public voice juries deliver. In 2026 he's gone further: stop asking and run one. The co-op thread runs through 888's earlier [Basil's Table](2023-01-21-podcast.md) panels. With a Victorian election two months away, the practical question from the evening is still open: what would it take for a standing citizens' assembly to exist in Australia, and who pays for the first one?
