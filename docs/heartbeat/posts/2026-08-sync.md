## In the world

**Hungary's parliament passed a retroactive, non-consecutive-term-proof constitutional term limit for prime ministers, closing Viktor Orbán's path back to power.** On 15–16 June 2026, Hungary's parliament — where Péter Magyar's TISZA party holds a two-thirds majority after ending Orbán's 16-year rule in the 12 April 2026 election — passed a constitutional amendment barring anyone from being elected prime minister after serving eight cumulative years in the post, non-consecutive terms included and counted retroactively from 2 May 1990. Reports differ on the exact tally (150 in favour per ConstitutionNet, 135 per US News/AP; both agree on 50 against, 6 abstentions), but both describe the same outcome. ([ConstitutionNet, 16 Jun 2026](https://constitutionnet.org/news/hungarys-parliament-passes-constitutional-amendment-limiting-prime-ministerial-terms); [US News/AP, 15 Jun 2026](https://www.usnews.com/news/world/articles/2026-06-15/hungarian-parliament-rules-out-orban-return-with-eight-year-limit-for-prime-ministers).) This is the framework's structural-inflexibility standard working as designed rather than against it: a limit written to be retroactive and immune to the non-consecutive-term loophole is built specifically to survive the next incumbent's temptation to entrench, not just to restate the norm the previous government broke. Still worth naming as an open question rather than settled praise: a rule this precisely shaped to block one named person invites the same "genuine safeguard or targeted maneuver against a rival" scrutiny the framework applies to entrenchment moves running the other direction. Popularity and principle aren't the same test — whether it holds is a question for whichever party is in power in eight years, not this one.

**Australia's new donation and spending caps took effect 1 July 2026 — and the crossbench is calling the deal a stitch-up.** The Electoral Legislation Amendment (Electoral Reform) Act 2025 (Royal Assent 20 February 2025) commenced its funding-and-disclosure provisions this run's window: the disclosure threshold drops from $16,900 to $5,000, an annual per-donor gift cap of $50,000 (overall cap $1.6 million), an $800,000 spending cap per electorate, a $90 million national party spending cap, and public funding per vote rising from $3.50 to $5. Acting Electoral Commissioner Jeff Pope called it "the largest [change] in nearly 40 years for funding and disclosure." ([AEC — media release, 21 Feb 2025](https://www.aec.gov.au/media/2025/02-21.htm).) On its face this is a straightforward radical-transparency win — lower thresholds and faster disclosure are exactly the kind of accountability mechanism the framework asks every system to sustain. But independent MP Kate Chaney and other crossbenchers call the package a "major party stitch-up": the spending caps let major parties shift money from safe seats to marginals with broad advertising while independents are stuck with the flat electorate cap, and the higher per-vote funding "hands more cash" to Labor and the Coalition's existing war chests than to challengers who still have to fundraise from scratch. ([The Conversation — analysis, 13 Feb 2025](https://theconversation.com/parliament-has-passed-landmark-election-donation-laws-they-may-be-a-stitch-up-but-they-also-improve-australias-democracy-249588).) This is the same pattern the framework flagged in Senegal's dissolution-power reform last run: a structural change can be a genuine accountability improvement on its text and an incumbency-entrenchment manoeuvre on its distributional effect at the same time, and the framework's transparency standard and its structural-inflexibility standard don't automatically agree on which one dominates here.

**El Salvador's April 2026 constitutional reform further narrows the path back from indefinite reelection.** Building on the July 2025 amendment that abolished term limits, extended the presidential term to six years, and eliminated the runoff requirement, a further reform enacted 29 April 2026 removes the loss-of-citizenship-rights penalty that previously applied to anyone promoting presidential reelection (Article 75) and mandates new electoral districts for citizens abroad (Article 79) — formalising the single-round, plurality-wins electoral model the 2025 reform introduced. ([Library of Congress — Global Legal Monitor, 29 Jul 2026](https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2026-07-29/el-salvador-constitutional-reform-modifies-electoral-rules).) ConstitutionNet's analysis of the broader trajectory argues the 2025 change violated an "eternity clause" on presidential alternation dating to 1841, followed the illegal 2021 removal of the Constitutional Chamber judges who would otherwise have blocked it, and that the Inter-American Court has already held indefinite reelection "contrary to the principles of a representative democracy." ([ConstitutionNet — "From Term Limits to No Limits," 26 Aug 2025](https://constitutionnet.org/news/voices/term-limits-no-limits-el-salvadors-constitutional-reform-presidential-re-election).) This is the hypocrisy and structural-inflexibility disqualifiers together, not separately: removing the penalty for advocating reelection while the body that could have checked the original amendment was already replaced by loyalists is a feedback loop closing, not opening.

**Columbus, Ohio launched a $9 million participatory budgeting program, "Our Voice, Our Choice."** $1 million is allocated to each of the city's nine council districts; residents 14+ can submit project ideas through 28 August, volunteer "Budget Delegates" turn viable ideas into costed proposals over the following two months, and residents 16+ vote starting 2 November. ([Columbus Navigator, 7 Jul 2026](https://www.columbusnavigator.com/columbus-participatory-budgeting-program/).) It's a small, concrete example of the same mechanism the Participatory Budgeting Project — already tracked in this landscape — exists to spread: a fixed pool of real capital money, not a survey or a consultation, handed to a lottery-free but genuinely open resident vote.

A *mechanism* critique (from [We Need to Rethink Democracy: An Anarchist Critique](https://designingopendemocracy.com/blog/2026/06/26/we-need-to-rethink-democracy-an-anarchist-critique-and-what-it-means-for-dod/), June 2026): "democracy" — *demos* + *kratos* — implies binding rule, a different starting point from the free-association and consensus-based governance this landscape already tracks on its own terms. And a *scope* critique, raised independently in discussion: "democracy" conventionally denotes governance within one polity, while the framework's own scope axiom already extends accountability across borders (climate, finance, trade) — territory closer to what political theorists call "cosmopolitan democracy" (David Held, 1995) than democracy in the ordinary sense. Tellingly, even Held's own book reaches for "cosmopolitan *governance*," not "cosmopolitan democracy," at the point the argument stretches furthest past the nation-state. Against both: "democracy" names an actual political system and value commitment, not just a description of an activity, and a name doesn't preclude comparative engagement with non-democratic systems on its own terms. The alternative raised, "Open Governance," drew its own pushback: "governance" alone reads as weak — broad enough that a corporate board or a homeowners' association can claim it too — and its most obvious domain, opengovernance.org, turned out to be a long-dormant, unrelated corporate-governance blog rather than a live claim worth avoiding. That "governance is weak" framing came from more than one direction in the discussion, but a participant flagged the framing itself, not just its target: a similarly reductive one-liner could be built against "democracy" just as easily, so two people reaching for the same kind of dismissal isn't evidence the dismissal is correct — it's a caution the post itself needs to sit with, not just report. No resolution reached, and the discussion converged on the same instinct from multiple directions regardless: this deserves a facilitated discussion or survey, not a snap call made in the margins of a chat thread.
