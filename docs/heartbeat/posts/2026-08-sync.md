## In the world

**Hungary's parliament passed a retroactive, non-consecutive-term-proof constitutional term limit for prime ministers, closing Viktor Orbán's path back to power.** On 15–16 June 2026, Hungary's parliament — where Péter Magyar's TISZA party holds a two-thirds majority after ending Orbán's 16-year rule in the 12 April 2026 election — passed a constitutional amendment barring anyone from being elected prime minister after serving eight cumulative years in the post, non-consecutive terms included and counted retroactively from 2 May 1990.[^hu-cn] Reports differ on the exact tally (150 in favour per ConstitutionNet,[^hu-cn] 135 per [US News/AP, 15 Jun 2026](https://www.usnews.com/news/world/articles/2026-06-15/hungarian-parliament-rules-out-orban-return-with-eight-year-limit-for-prime-ministers); both agree on 50 against, 6 abstentions), but both describe the same outcome. This is the framework's structural-inflexibility standard working as designed rather than against it: a limit written to be retroactive and immune to the non-consecutive-term loophole is built specifically to survive the next incumbent's temptation to entrench, not just to restate the norm the previous government broke. Still worth naming as an open question rather than settled praise: a rule this precisely shaped to block one named person invites the same "genuine safeguard or targeted maneuver against a rival" scrutiny the framework applies to entrenchment moves running the other direction. Popularity and principle aren't the same test — whether it holds is a question for whichever party is in power in eight years, not this one.

**Australia's new donation and spending caps took effect 1 July 2026 — and the crossbench sees the design as entrenching the major parties.** The Electoral Legislation Amendment (Electoral Reform) Act 2025 (Royal Assent 20 February 2025) commenced its funding-and-disclosure provisions this run's window: the disclosure threshold drops from $16,900 to $5,000, an annual per-donor gift cap of $50,000 (overall cap $1.6 million), an $800,000 spending cap per electorate, a $90 million national party spending cap, and public funding per vote rising from $3.50 to $5. Acting Electoral Commissioner Jeff Pope said the amendments are "substantial – the largest in nearly 40 years for funding and disclosure."[^au-aec] On its face this is a straightforward radical-transparency win — lower thresholds and faster disclosure are exactly the kind of accountability mechanism the framework asks every system to sustain. But independent MP Kate Chaney frames the effect as entrenching incumbents: "The effect of increasing public funding is that political parties don’t have to fundraise because they’ve got their war chests. But any challengers do have to fundraise."[^au-conv] The Conversation's own headline captures the tension crossbenchers see in the package — that it may be a "stitch up" even as it also improves Australia's democracy.[^au-conv] This is the same pattern the framework flagged in Senegal's dissolution-power reform last run: a structural change can be a genuine accountability improvement on its text and an incumbency-entrenchment manoeuvre on its distributional effect at the same time, and the framework's transparency standard and its structural-inflexibility standard don't automatically agree on which one dominates here.

**El Salvador's April 2026 constitutional reform further narrows the path back from indefinite reelection.** Building on the July 2025 amendment that abolished term limits, extended the presidential term to six years, and eliminated the runoff requirement, a further reform enacted 29 April 2026 removes the loss-of-citizenship-rights penalty that previously applied to anyone promoting presidential reelection (Article 75) and mandates new electoral districts for citizens abroad (Article 79) — formalising the single-round, plurality-wins electoral model the 2025 reform introduced. ([Library of Congress — Global Legal Monitor, 29 Jul 2026](https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2026-07-29/el-salvador-constitutional-reform-modifies-electoral-rules).) ConstitutionNet's analysis of the broader trajectory notes the constitution's Article 248 bars amending its clauses on presidential alternation — an "eternity clause" dating to 1841 — and that the amendment followed the Legislative Assembly's removal, on 1 May 2021, of "all magistrates of the Constitutional Chamber," replacing them the same day "without following the selection procedure set out in Article 186 of the Constitution."[^sv-cn] The Inter-American Court has separately held that "enabling indefinite presidential re-election is contrary to the principles of a representative democracy and, therefore, to the obligations established in the American Convention."[^sv-cn] This is the hypocrisy and structural-inflexibility disqualifiers together, not separately: removing the penalty for advocating reelection while the body that could have checked the original amendment was already replaced by loyalists is a feedback loop closing, not opening.

**Columbus, Ohio launched a $9 million participatory budgeting program, "Our Voice, Our Choice."** "The pilot program sets aside $9 million from the city’s capital budget, with $1 million allocated to each of Columbus’ nine City Council districts."[^col] "Residents 14 and older can submit project ideas" through 28 August, volunteer "Budget Delegates" turn viable ideas into costed proposals over the following two months, and "anyone 16 or older who lives in a City Council district can vote on the final proposals for their district," starting 2 November.[^col] It's a small, concrete example of the same mechanism the Participatory Budgeting Project — already tracked in this landscape — exists to spread: a fixed pool of real capital money, not a survey or a consultation, handed to a lottery-free but genuinely open resident vote.

**Pakistan's information ministry issued new rules requiring foreign correspondents to obtain government permission before reporting outside three cities, days after accusing Al Jazeera of "yellow journalism."** The "Foreign Media Facilitation Guidelines 2026" require international correspondents — and Pakistani journalists contributing to foreign outlets — to obtain a no-objection certificate before working outside Islamabad, Lahore, or Karachi, and let authorities suspend or revoke accreditation for "acts against the ideology, sovereignty, security or public order of Pakistan."[^pk] The guidelines followed the government's public criticism of international coverage of Kashmir election unrest. The Committee to Protect Journalists called them "yet another appalling blow to press freedom in Pakistan, where journalists are already facing an intense crackdown."[^pk] This one doesn't need much hedging against the framework: a discretionary suspension trigger this vague — "security or public order," defined solely by the body being reported on — timed to coverage that body had just publicly objected to, is close to the textbook case of a state shielding its own conduct from scrutiny rather than a defensible security measure.

**US Treasury permanently exempted over 99% of American companies from beneficial-ownership reporting under the Corporate Transparency Act, and ordered the ownership data already filed to be deleted.** FinCEN's final rule, effective 14 August 2026, eliminates the reporting requirement for US-formed companies and US persons, narrowing the law to foreign reporting companies only. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent called it "a victory for common sense and American small businesses."[^us-treasury] Senators Sheldon Whitehouse and Chuck Grassley — the law's own Democratic and Republican co-authors — issued a joint statement calling it an undermining of "the clear intent of the law."[^us-wg] Beneficial-ownership registries are one of the more concrete anti-corruption mechanisms this landscape tracks — the tool that shows who actually owns a shell company, not just its nominal directors. The deletion is the sharper part: this isn't relaxing a requirement going forward, it's unwinding transparency that already existed. Worth naming plainly: this isn't DOD's framework lining up with one side of a partisan fight — the two senators making the accountability argument against this rule wrote the law it weakens.

**Nicaragua's Ortega moved to formally abolish elections, extend the presidential term to seven years, and bar 'traitors' from running.** Five days after Daniel Ortega declared during a 19 July 2026 revolution-anniversary speech that Nicaragua "will never again hold elections" that would let the opposition "return to power," his government opened the formal process of reforming the Constitution and electoral law on 24 July 2026.[^ni-upi] A draft constitutional reform sent to the National Assembly the following week confirmed the shape of the change: National Assembly President Gustavo Porras read out a proposal to reorganise the presidency "with an effective term of seven renewable years," up from the current six, and the reform separately aims to exclude "traitors" and "coup-plotting" opposition members from elections.[^ni-aj] The reform is expected to pass in September 2026. This is the structural-inflexibility and hypocrisy disqualifiers stacked, not separately: a sitting government using its own constitutional-amendment power to write itself an unbounded, self-defined veto over who its opponents are allowed to be is close to the limiting case the framework's scope axiom exists to name — accountability isn't just weakened here, the mechanism for restoring it is being removed from the document that would otherwise require it. Unlike the Hungary item above, there's no term-limit-style safeguard being built here to survive a future officeholder; there's a sitting officeholder writing the rules that keep him one.

## Framework notes

**On the org's own name — still an open discussion, not a run decision.** The internal conversation noted here earlier this month kept going and sharpened into two separable critiques of "Designing Open Democracy," worth keeping apart rather than treating as one "too narrow" complaint. A *mechanism* critique (from [We Need to Rethink Democracy: An Anarchist Critique](https://designingopendemocracy.com/blog/2026/06/26/we-need-to-rethink-democracy-an-anarchist-critique-and-what-it-means-for-dod/), June 2026): "democracy" — *demos* + *kratos* — implies binding rule, a different starting point from the free-association and consensus-based governance this landscape already tracks on its own terms. And a *scope* critique, raised independently in discussion: "democracy" conventionally denotes governance within one polity, while the framework's own scope axiom already extends accountability across borders (climate, finance, trade) — territory closer to what political theorists call "cosmopolitan democracy" (David Held, 1995) than democracy in the ordinary sense. Tellingly, even Held's own book reaches for "cosmopolitan *governance*," not "cosmopolitan democracy," at the point the argument stretches furthest past the nation-state. Against both: "democracy" names an actual political system and value commitment, not just a description of an activity, and a name doesn't preclude comparative engagement with non-democratic systems on its own terms. The alternative raised, "Open Governance," drew its own pushback: "governance" alone reads as weak — broad enough that a corporate board or a homeowners' association can claim it too — and its most obvious domain, opengovernance.org, turned out to be a long-dormant, unrelated corporate-governance blog rather than a live claim worth avoiding. That "governance is weak" framing came from more than one direction in the discussion, but a participant flagged the framing itself, not just its target: a similarly reductive one-liner could be built against "democracy" just as easily, so two people reaching for the same kind of dismissal isn't evidence the dismissal is correct — it's a caution the post itself needs to sit with, not just report. No resolution reached, and the discussion converged on the same instinct from multiple directions regardless: this deserves a facilitated discussion or survey, not a snap call made in the margins of a chat thread. Recording the shape of the disagreement here for whenever that happens, not a verdict.
