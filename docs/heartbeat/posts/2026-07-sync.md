## In the world

**Ukraine's first metropolitan citizens' assembly concludes in Lviv with 19 recommendations.** The Lviv Agglomeration assembly — convened across 13 communities to recommend how to manage shared water resources — ran its opening session on 13–14 June 2026, its second on 27–28 June, and its final session on 11–12 July, where the 50 lot-selected members (half from Lviv, half from the 12 partner communities) delivered 19 practical proposals spanning joint water-management frameworks, sewerage infrastructure, ecological protection, and public-awareness campaigns. Local leaders received the recommendations with what the Council of Europe describes as positive feedback and have begun outlining first implementation steps. It's organised by the Council of Europe's Congress of Local and Regional Authorities alongside Kyiv and Rivne assemblies running in parallel. ([Council of Europe — Congress](https://www.coe.int/en/web/congress/-/ukraine-s-first-metropolitan-citizens-assembly-launched-in-lviv); [Mirage News — final outcome](https://www.miragenews.com/lviv-assembly-yields-19-water-protection-1710540/).) This extends the deliberative-democracy thread already in the landscape — wartime Ukraine ran a sortition-based assembly at inter-municipal scale through to a concrete set of recommendations, not just a single-city pilot, while under active invasion.

**V-Dem's 2026 Democracy Report finds 44 countries autocratizing — a record share of the world's population — with six of ten new entrants in Europe and North America.** The new 2025 autocratizers are Cambodia, Croatia, Italy, Kuwait, Madagascar, Slovakia, Slovenia, Togo, the UK, and the US; V-Dem's press release calls the US decline "unprecedented" for a long-established democracy. The specific indicators driving the score — media censorship (32 of 44 countries), civil-society repression (30 of 44), and worsening freedom of expression (44 countries) — are structural, not electoral: they measure whether avenues for dissent and oversight stay open between elections, which is exactly the kind of question the accountability framework's structural-inflexibility test asks of every entry in this landscape, liberal democracies included. ([V-Dem Institute — press release](https://www.v-dem.net/news/press-release-democratic-backsliding-reaches-western-democracies-with-us-decline-unprecedented/); [full report, PDF](https://v-dem.net/documents/75/V-Dem_Institute_Democracy_Report_2026_lowres.pdf).) The framework says treat every system by the same yardstick — this is that yardstick turned on the wealthiest democracies in the sample, not just the usual subjects.

**A different index reached the opposite headline the same year.** The Economist Intelligence Unit's Democracy Index, in the full report it released on 7 April 2026, found the global average score rising for the first time in eight years (5.17 to 5.19), classifying 26 countries as full democracies, 48 as flawed, 32 as hybrid regimes, and 61 as authoritarian across 167 countries and territories. ([EIU — Democracy Index 2025 press release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/eiu-democracy-index-2025-democracy-stabilises-after-eight-years-of-decline-302734863.html).) That's a genuinely different top-line story from V-Dem's record-backsliding framing above — yet on the one country both indices call out by name, they agree: the EIU places the US 34th with a score of 7.65, down from 7.85, its lowest since being downgraded to "flawed democracy" in 2016, attributed to the same functioning-of-government and civil-liberties weaknesses V-Dem flags. Two indices measuring democracy differently can both be right at once; citing only the one with the more dramatic headline would be the kind of selective framing the framework asks us not to practice on ourselves.

**Connecticut's first state-level citizens' assembly convened its opening session on schedule.** After the Connecticut Lottery Corporation drew members in a public lottery on 12 June 2026, 100 residents opened the assembly with a two-day in-person session at Yale on 11–12 July, the first of six sessions running through 26 September — split between in-person days in New Haven and Hartford and virtual sessions — to develop recommendations on how the state funds local public services, a problem the state has not substantively reformed since 1972. It's convened jointly by the State Comptroller's office, the Connecticut Conference of Municipalities, Yale, and UConn, with the Comptroller committing to present the assembly's findings directly to a State Legislature hearing. ([Connecticut Citizens' Assembly — official site](https://ct-citizens-assembly.org/); [CT Mirror — opening session](https://ctmirror.org/2026/07/13/citizens-assembly-property-taxes/).) It's a useful contrast to the Ukraine and EU assemblies above: a citizens' assembly convened by elected officials, inside an established liberal democracy, on a genuinely difficult distributive question that ordinary electoral politics has failed to resolve for over 50 years — exactly the kind of case for sortition-based deliberation that doesn't depend on a system being in crisis or being non-Western to be worth doing.

**DemocracyNext published lessons from four years advising cities and regions on institutionalizing citizens' assemblies.** *From Projects to Permanence*, released 15 July 2026, draws on DemocracyNext's Cities Programme work with Esch-sur-Alzette (Luxembourg), Vilnius, Kerewan (The Gambia), and Deschutes County (Oregon) to argue assemblies should be built as standing institutions with power delegated to them, not one-off processes dependent on a mayor's term or political will at a single moment — its central line is to treat the work "like a marathon, not a sprint." ([DemocracyNext — paper announcement](https://demnext.substack.com/p/new-paper-from-projects-to-permanence).) DemocracyNext is already tracked in this landscape; this is exactly the kind of institutional-design question the accountability framework's structural-inflexibility test cares about from the other direction — a citizens' assembly that exists for one cycle and then evaporates is no more structurally resilient than any other reform that depends on one administration's goodwill to survive the next.

**Kazakhstan's new constitution took effect on 1 July 2026, replacing the 1995 document.** Approved by referendum in March (nearly 90% in favour, 73% turnout), it abolishes the Senate for a single 145-seat Kurultai, adds a vice presidency, and creates a 126-member Halyk Kenesi ("People's Council") empowered to submit draft laws and initiate referenda. ([Astana Times — what changes](https://astanatimes.com/2026/07/kazakhstans-new-constitution-takes-effect-heres-what-changes/); [Library of Congress — structural summary](https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2026-05-27/kazakhstan-new-constitution-adopted-following-nationwide-referendum).) One provision cuts against incumbency entrenchment directly: the president is limited to a single, non-renewable seven-year term, textually entrenched against future amendment — a genuine answer to the structural-inflexibility pattern the framework flags elsewhere in this landscape. But the People's Council's entire membership is presidentially appointed, and the same package gives the president power to dissolve the Kurultai over repeated nominee rejections, rule by decree while it's dissolved, and appoint key officials without parliamentary approval. ConstitutionNet's analysis calls the parliamentary reform "a façade for presidential monopoly." ([ConstitutionNet — analysis](https://constitutionnet.org/news/voices/new-constitution-kazakhstan-parliamentary-reform-facade-presidential-monopoly).) A wholly appointed consultative body that can propose but not check power is the same "bounded articulation" pattern the framework already uses to describe China's CPPCC — worth naming here rather than reserving the label for one country.
