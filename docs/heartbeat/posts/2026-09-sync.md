## In the world

### Niger's junta survives a coup attempt — with Russian help

In the early hours of 29 August 2026, hundreds of soldiers — reportedly including special-forces officers — struck the presidential palace, Air Base 101, the national broadcaster, and a hotel in Niamey, attempting to overthrow junta leader Abdourahamane Tiani, who himself took power in a July 2023 coup.[^ne-ct] Loyalist forces retook the air base "the evening of August 29" only "with support from around 200 Russian Africa Corps soldiers and Russian drone support"; Algeria separately flew in fighter jets the next day in what the analysis calls its first foreign military deployment since 1973.[^ne-ct] The fighting killed at least 27 people.[^ne-ct] A Sahel researcher told RFI the coordinated targeting pointed to an organised coup attempt rather than a mutiny that escalated, and put the outcome plainly: "I don't think it's an exaggeration to say that the Russians saved the day, or at least helped contain the rebellion and the attempted coup."[^ne-rfi]

The framework's trust clause is genuinely silent on cases like this, and worth naming as silent rather than stretched to a verdict. Tiani's government was never accountable to Nigeriens by the framework's own good-faith test to begin with, so foreign paramilitary support here isn't a case of an outside actor overriding a functioning system — it's a case of outside force being the reason an already-unaccountable arrangement is still standing at all. That's a different problem than the trust clause was written to name (coercive tools used *against* a system, for good or ill), and this landscape's framework doesn't currently have a test for it: what to say about a government's legitimacy when its survival, not just its formation, now runs through a foreign military's operational choices.

### A citizen referendum forces Missouri back to its old congressional map — for now

Missouri's Republican-controlled legislature passed a mid-decade congressional map in 2025, widely reported as intended to redraw a Democratic-held Kansas City seat into Republican hands. Opponents used the state's citizen-referendum mechanism, gathering more than 300,000 signatures to force a public vote — which under Missouri law suspends a challenged law pending that vote. On 3 September 2026 the Missouri Supreme Court ruled the referendum petition "legal, sufficient, and timely," and held that the new map "did not go into effect and will not go into effect unless and until approved by the voters."[^mo-scotus] A federal district judge ordered the new map used anyway for November, reasoning from continuity with candidates already nominated under it in the August primary; on 10 September the U.S. Supreme Court stayed that order, without stated reasoning, leaving the state court's ruling in place. The practical result: Missouri's 2022 map, not the 2025 one, is expected to govern the November election, while the referendum itself still goes to voters and further litigation continues.[^mo-scotus-stay]

**Why it matters.** This is a live test of whether a state's own citizen-referendum power — a direct-democracy correction mechanism written into its constitution — actually binds a legislature's map, or can be routed around through a separate court's intervening order. So far it has held: neither the legislature's own act nor a federal district court's order has displaced the referendum's suspending effect, and the U.S. Supreme Court has twice declined, on its emergency docket, to disturb that outcome.

**Framework lens.** The accountability framework's structural-inflexibility disqualifier is about feedback loops to power that are broken or blocked. This is the inverse case worth naming when it happens, not just when it fails: a real feedback loop being tested against exactly the kind of procedural end-run that would make it hollow, and surviving so far. That's worth flagging as provisional rather than settled — the underlying case continues, and nothing here says which map governs future cycles, only that for this one, the correction mechanism functioned as designed.

### A military junta rewrites Guinea-Bissau's constitution — then bars itself from the presidency it just strengthened

On 30 August 2026, Guinea-Bissau held a referendum on a new constitution drafted by the military government that seized power in a November 2025 coup.[^gnb-context] The National Electoral Commission announced on 1 September that 70% of voters approved it.[^gnb-referendum] The new constitution lets the president appoint and dismiss the prime minister and cabinet, create or abolish ministries, chair the Council of Ministers, and dissolve parliament in a "grave political crisis," while shrinking the National People's Assembly from 102 to 65 seats and its constituencies from 29 to 12. Opposition parties, including the PAIGC, called for a boycott, warning the changes would weaken the prime minister, the government, political parties and the legislature.[^gnb-referendum] The junta's own transitional charter separately bars its leader, General Horta Inta-A Na Man, from standing in the general election it has set for 6 December.[^gnb-election]

**Why it matters.** This isn't the familiar "coup leader entrenches himself" story — Horta is barred from running. What's being entrenched instead is the office itself: an unelected, temporary authority permanently rewrote the constitutional balance of power on its own timetable, then handed the strengthened result to whoever an election it hasn't held yet elects.

**Framework lens.** The accountability framework's structural-inflexibility disqualifier turns on whether a system's feedback loops to power stay open. A referendum is normally exactly that kind of loop — but one drafted, timed and run entirely by the same unaccountable body whose own exit it is negotiating is a different thing from voters correcting a government they elected. It sits next to this month's other two items as a third variation on the same underlying question: Niger's coup survived on a foreign military's choices, Missouri's map fight tested whether a citizen mechanism can bind an elected legislature, and here an unelected authority pre-writes the rulebook for whichever elected government follows it. All three are about who a system of power actually answers to, from different angles.

### DRC parliament weighs a route around its own term-limit lock <!-- tentative: revisit next run -->

The Congolese constitution explicitly bars any revision of presidential term limits. A bill now before the National Assembly would carve an exception into that lock: it would let the president amend those provisions in cases of "major dysfunction" paralysing state institutions, subject to a referendum.[^drc-ap] President Félix Tshisekedi — due to complete his constitutional second term in 2028 — has said he "would be willing to seek a third term if approved by voters in a referendum."[^drc-ap] On 15 September 2026, the opposition C64 coalition organised a nationwide day of action against the bill; police fired tear gas and deployed heavily in Kinshasa, with further demonstrations in Lubumbashi and Uvira despite restrictions, and at least 10 arrests reported. Opposition figure Mohamed Eyenga said activists "were unfortunately and arbitrarily arrested," while a provincial police commander said no "peaceful" demonstrators had been detained.[^drc-aj] A June 2026 opposition rally against the same push was also broken up by police, injuring several people including opposition leader Martin Fayulu.[^drc-ap]

**Why it might matter.** A constitution's explicit, unamendable term-limit clause is about as hard a structural check as a text can write — the same kind of "feedback loop that's supposed to stay closed to self-dealing" the framework's structural-inflexibility language is built around. What's notable here isn't a leader ignoring that check; it's a bill designed to open a lawful door through it, with a referendum offered as the legitimising mechanism — a referendum the incumbent himself would be the direct beneficiary of, which is a different shape from Missouri's referendum-as-check-on-power or Guinea-Bissau's referendum-then-self-exclusion earlier in this post. Whether that reads as constitutional self-dealing or a legitimate emergency-provision safety valve turns on facts not yet settled — has the National Assembly actually passed the bill, and what "major dysfunction" ends up meaning in the text.

Marked tentative because the bill's status in the Assembly, and whether the protest movement grows or fades, are both still open as of this pass — worth a firmer read once the legislative process moves rather than reacting to the first protest.
